Resume for career changers: how to show you can do the new job

A career-change resume has one job: make it easy for a recruiter — and an applicant tracking system — to see that your past work already used the skills the new role needs. Use a hybrid format (a skills-led summary on top, normal reverse-chronological experience below), translate your old job’s language into the new field’s words, and lead every bullet with the transferable part.

Transferable skills: translate your experience

Recruiters and keyword searches use the vocabulary of the new field. Rewrite your experience in that vocabulary, keeping every claim true.

Summary examples for a career change

The summary is where you connect the old and the new. Formula: target title + transferable strength + proof + new skill you have added.

Teacher → instructional designer
“Instructional designer with 7 years of classroom experience designing and assessing curriculum for middle-school learners. Completed a certificate in e-learning design; builds courses in Articulate Storyline and applies ADDIE.”

Retail manager → project coordinator
“Project coordinator with 6 years leading store operations: scheduling, budgets, vendor coordination and team of 18. Trained in Asana and Smartsheet; CAPM candidate.”

Accountant → data analyst
“Data analyst with a background in financial reporting. Uses SQL, Excel and Power BI to build reports that finance teams act on; automated a monthly reconciliation that saved 10 hours a month.”

Use the new job title only if you can back it with training, projects or real overlapping work. Otherwise, write “Former teacher moving into instructional design…”.

Hybrid resume format, step by step

  1. Header and target title. Name, city, phone, email, LinkedIn — in the page body. Then the title of the role you want.
  2. Summary (3 lines, see above).
  3. Key skills — 6 to 10 skills from the job posts, grouped on plain lines. Only skills you can talk about in an interview.
  4. Relevant projects or training — courses, certificates, volunteer or freelance work in the new field. Treat each project like a job entry: title, dates, two bullets with results.
  5. Experience — reverse-chronological, with titles, employers and dates. Rewrite bullets so the transferable part comes first.
  6. Education and certifications.

Why hybrid and not a pure “functional” resume: a skills-only resume with no dates hides your timeline, which many recruiters distrust, and parsers expect job titles and dates to calculate experience. The hybrid gives the skills focus while keeping a normal job history.

Making a career-change resume ATS-readable

  • Use the new field’s exact keywords where they truthfully describe your work.
  • Keep standard headings: Summary, Skills, Projects, Experience, Education.
  • One column, no tables or text boxes for key content, dates in one format.
  • Do not invent job titles. Keep your real title and describe the work in the new vocabulary.
  • Expect to answer screening questions honestly — no layout trick changes those.

Common career-change mistakes

  • Opening with an apology (“Although I don’t have direct experience…”). Lead with what you do have.
  • Listing every duty from the old job. Keep the bullets that map to the new one.
  • Hiding dates. Gaps and switches are normal; missing dates raise more questions.
  • One generic resume for every application. Tailor the summary and skills each time.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use a functional resume to change careers?

Usually a hybrid works better. It puts skills first but keeps job titles and dates, which recruiters and applicant tracking systems both look for.

Do I need a certificate before I apply?

Not always. Projects, freelance work and on-the-job overlap count. A recognised certificate helps when job posts list it as a requirement.

How far back should I go?

Roughly ten to fifteen years in detail. Older roles can be one line each, unless they are directly relevant to the new field.

Can an ATS score tell me if I will get the job?

        Other things worth a look

          The checker only sees plain text. It cannot tell whether your file uses tables, columns, text boxes, a header or footer, or images, so check those yourself (see the list below).

          How the match rate is worked out

          1. Keywords from the advert. The advert is split into words. Common English words (“the”, “and”, “with”) and words found in almost every advert (“role”, “team”, “candidate”, “experience”) are dropped. Two- and three-word phrases such as “stakeholder management” are kept when they appear at least twice (a longer phrase replaces a shorter one inside it, unless the shorter one appears more often), or once if they look like a named skill (for example “Microsoft Excel”). Single words are kept when they appear at least twice, or once if they are an acronym or a tool name such as SQL, CIPD or C#.
          2. Weighting. Each keyword is weighted by how often the advert uses it, capped at 3, so one word repeated ten times cannot swamp the rest. Phrases, acronyms and tool names get a small bonus. Up to 30 keywords are used.
          3. Matching. A keyword counts as found if your CV contains it, ignoring capitals and simple plural endings (“stakeholder” matches “stakeholders”). A different form of the word (“managed” for “management”), or a phrase whose words are all in your CV but not side by side, is marked “partly” and counts for half.
          4. Match rate = weight of keywords found ÷ total weight of the advert’s keywords. It is a rough guide to coverage, not a prediction of what any employer’s system will do.
          5. Headings. Short lines on their own are compared with common CV headings: Profile (or Personal statement, Summary), Work experience (or Employment history), Education (or Qualifications), Skills, and References.

          What applicant tracking systems struggle with

          From the public guidance listed under Sources:

          • Keywords from the advert. Prospects notes that ATS software screens CVs for keywords from the job description, and advises reading the job description and person specification and including the relevant keywords, with qualifications and certifications identified by name.
          • Images and graphics. Prospects says to avoid images, logos and graphics because an ATS cannot read them. Greenhouse lists graphics, photos and word art, and CVs uploaded as an image, among the causes of a failed parse.
          • Tables, columns and text boxes. Greenhouse also lists tables, headers and footers, contact details placed in a header, footer or text box, and columned layouts.
          • Clear sections and full job titles. Greenhouse mentions CVs without clear sections, letters spaced out across a word, and shortened titles such as “Sr. Account Exec” instead of “Senior Account Executive”.
          • File type. Prospects points out that not every ATS accepts PDF, so check which format the employer asks for.
          • UK CV sections. The National Careers Service suggests contact details, a short introduction, education, work history and references, and says not to include your age, date of birth, marital status or nationality.

          Sources

          • Prospects: How to write a CV (section on applicant tracking systems)
          • Greenhouse Support: Unsuccessful resume parse
          • National Careers Service: How to write a CV

          Every employer’s system is set up differently. A high match rate does not guarantee an interview, and a low one does not mean you will be rejected; it just shows where your wording and the advert’s differ.
